Modeling Strong Interaction within a
Hydrodynamic Model of Quantum Vacuum:
A Qualitative Study
Dmitry Losinets
October 2025
Abstract
Within the framework of the program exploring the possibility of a unified hy-
drodynamic description of physical phenomena [1–4], a qualitative modeling of the
strong (nuclear) interaction is conducted. The interaction between nucleons is mod-
eled as the result of vortex-vortex flow interactions in an ideal incompressible fluid.
It is shown that in a system of two cylindrical vortices at short distances, forces
emerge that qualitatively resemble strong interactions: attraction at intermediate
ranges and repulsion at ultra-short ones (“hard-core” behavior). For the first time
within this model, a significant dependence of the interaction on the relative ori-
entation of vortex axes is considered, allowing the vortex angular momentum to
be associated with spin. The influence of multi-vortex geometry on short-range
interaction is discussed. Quantitative parameter fitting and rigorous justification
are beyond the scope of this work and are left for further study. The model draws
on classical vortex dynamics, notably the work of Helmholtz (1858) on point vor-
tices [5], and on recent research including vortex excitations in nuclei observed in
58Ni (2025) [6], as well as hydrodynamic analogs in quantum mechanics [7].
1
Introduction
In works [1–4], a hydrodynamic model of the quantum vacuum was developed, represent-
ing it as a weakly viscous fluid. Within this model, consistent analogs for electromag-
netism, gravitation, photon polarization, and several quantum-mechanical effects were
obtained. These successes suggest the possibility of unifying fundamental interactions
within a single classical hydrodynamic framework, reminiscent of Kelvin’s vortex atom
hypothesis (1867) [8] and modern hydrodynamic interpretations of quantum mechanics
through turbulence [9].
The goal of this paper is to explore the feasibility of modeling the strong (nuclear)
interaction within this paradigm. We aim to demonstrate that qualitative features of
this interaction, attraction at distances ∼1 fm, replaced by strong repulsion at smaller
distances, spin dependence, and saturation, can naturally arise from the dynamics of vor-
tex structures. Special attention is paid to the role of vortex orientation and geometrical
constraints in multi-vortex systems, consistent with recent studies on vortex models of
nuclear forces, including pulsar glitch observations [10].
1
For the transition from speculative analogy to testable physical hypothesis, we propose
three potential experiments based on key model predictions, spin dependence, geometric
effects on nuclear stability, and collective vortex behavior, providing a bridge between
qualitative theory and empirical verification, in line with existing experiments on spin-
dependent nucleon-nucleon interactions [11].

Spin Dependence and Geometry
8.1
Vortex Orientation (Spin)
Vortices possess an intrinsic axis, and their angular momentum corresponds to spin [2,3].
Interaction efficiency critically depends on their relative orientation. Parallel vortices
attract strongly, while anti-parallel or skewed ones may produce anisotropic forces or
even repulsion [19].
• With parallel axes the model presented above is adequate.
• With antiparallel or crossed axes the geometry of the flow changes dramatically.
The flow induced by one vortex will not flow axisymmetrically around the second
one, but will twist around its axis at an angle. This will lead to a strong anisotropy
of the force and its possible weakening or even repulsion in some configurations.
Thus, the model predicts a significant dependence of the nuclear interaction on
the spins of interacting particles, in accordance with the latest searches for spin-
dependent interactions [20].
4
8.2
Neighboring Vortices and Saturation
In dense configurations, geometric constraints lead to:
• Screening: Long-range components are blocked by nearby vortices.
• Saturation: Each vortex interacts primarily with its nearest neighbors, explaining
the saturation of nuclear forces.
This qualitatively explains the property of saturation of nuclear forces, similar to the
effects in strongly interacting bose gases [21].

Proposed Experimental Verification
To move from a qualitative analogy to a testable physical hypothesis, we outline three
experiments grounded in the model’s key predictions - spin dependence, geometric effects
on nuclear stability, and collective vortex behavior.
These proposals are intended to
bridge theory and measurement, in line with existing spin-dependent nucleon–nucleon
studies [11].
9.1
Experiment 1: Spin-Dependent Nucleon Scattering
Hypothesis. The strength of the strong interaction between nucleons critically depends
on the relative orientation of their spins (vortex axes), not merely on separation. In
certain configurations (e.g., antiparallel spins), attraction may weaken or even turn into
repulsion, beyond what standard Yukawa-type potentials would predict to the same ex-
tent [22,23].
Setup.
1. Beam and target: Use polarized neutron beams and polarized proton targets (or
vice versa).
Modern techniques allow preparation of both polarized beams and
targets.
2. Configurations: Perform elastic-scattering runs at the same collision energy but
with different relative spin orientations:
(a) Configuration A: parallel spins (beam and target).
(b) Configuration B: antiparallel spins.
(c) Configuration C: unpolarized (control).
3. Observable: Measure the differential cross section dσ/dΩwith high precision at
small scattering angles, which probe the intermediate range (∼1–2 fm) where the
model predicts enhanced attraction for favorable orientations.
Expected result (per the model).
• Statistically significant differences in dσ/dΩbetween Configurations A and B.
• For parallel spins (most coaxial vortices), a larger cross section, indicating stronger
attraction.
5
• For antiparallel spins, a smaller cross section, consistent with reduced attraction due
to suboptimal flow geometry.
A clear spin-orientation dependence would directly support the proposal that flow geom-
etry (the spin analog) plays a fundamental role in nuclear forces [22,23].
9.2
Experiment 2: Geometric Anomalies in Light Nuclei
Hypothesis. Nuclear binding energy and stability depend not only on nucleon count
but also on spatial and spin orientations, which control how efficiently vortex “flows”
interpenetrate. Certain geometrically “degenerate” configurations should be unstable,
consistent with observations of vortex-like excitations in light nuclei such as 58Ni [6].
Setup.
1. Systems: Focus on exotic, weakly bound light nuclei where geometric effects are
most pronounced (e.g., 10He or neutron-halo systems).
2. Methods: Employ high-resolution nuclear spectroscopy (e.g., laser spectroscopy,
precise moment measurements) to extract:
(a) Nuclear density radii,
(b) Binding energies,
(c) Spins and parities of low-lying states.
3. Analysis: Compare with standard nuclear models (potential-based) and with qual-
itative predictions of the hydrodynamic vortex picture.
The model anticipates
anomalously low binding when vortex axes block each other’s flows (e.g., multiple
vortices with mutually perpendicular axes confined within a small volume).
Expected result (per the model).
• Identification of states anomalously unstable relative to standard predictions, whose
reconstructed spin geometry matches “flow-inefficient” configurations in the hydro-
dynamic analogy.
• Qualitative guidance on the most stable spin arrangements in few-body systems
(e.g., triton 3H, α particle 4He) that can be tested against precision data.
9.3
Experiment 3: Hydrodynamic Correlations in Relativistic
Heavy-Ion Collisions
Hypothesis. If nucleons and nuclei behave as vortices in a fluid, then the medium created
in high-energy nuclear collisions (QGP) should inherit and amplify these hydrodynamic
features. In particular, the initial vortex structure of the colliding nuclei should influence
final-state observables, in harmony with QGP hydrodynamics and vortex-related photon
phenomena [24].
Setup.
1. Conditions: Collide heavy ions (e.g., Au, Pb) at RHIC or the LHC.
6
2. Methodology: Use event-by-event analyses, categorizing events by initial collision
geometry and ideally by global spin orientation of the nuclei (assuming feasible
nuclear polarization at injection).
3. Observables: Study two-particle and multi-particle correlations and collective-flow
coefficients, especially directed (v1) and elliptic (v2) flow. The hydrodynamic model
predicts enhanced and structured vortex-like signals if the colliding ions possess
nonzero global spin alignment.
Expected result (per the model).
• Observation of anomalously strong collective behavior (e.g., v2 or vortex components
in v1) not fully captured by standard hydrodynamic initial-state density gradients
alone [24].
• Correlations between global spin orientation (if controllable) and the pattern of
angular correlations in final-state particles, consistent with a vortex-nature imprint
at extreme conditions, echoing recent vortex-laser style results [17].

Conclusion
Within the broader program aimed at developing a unified hydrodynamic model of phys-
ical phenomena [1–4], this work has carried out a qualitative investigation into the feasi-
bility of describing the strong nuclear interaction using vortex dynamics in an ideal fluid.
The analysis demonstrates that the interaction between finite-size vortices naturally re-
produces the key qualitative features of nuclear forces:
1. A strong attractive component at distances of about 1 fm, with an approximate
radial dependence ∼1/r3;
2. A short-range repulsive component (“hard-core” behavior) at r ≲a;
3. A pronounced dependence on vortex orientation, interpreted as a spin depen-
dence of the effective interaction;
4. The emergence of saturation effects due to geometric flow screening in multi-
vortex systems.
A major outcome of this study is the formulation of specific directions for possible
experimental validation of the model. The proposed experimental programs include:
• investigations of spin-dependent nucleon–nucleon scattering on polarized targets,
• the search for geometric anomalies and stability deviations in light nuclei,
• and the study of vortex-like hydrodynamic correlations in relativistic heavy-ion
collisions.
These proposed tests provide a bridge between the qualitative hydrodynamic frame-
work and empirically verifiable predictions. A critical test of the model would be the
observation of distinctive signatures such as a strong spin-orientation dependence of the
scattering cross section, consistent with stochastic vacuum models [13].
7
This work therefore demonstrates the principled feasibility of describing the strong
interaction hydrodynamically and outlines clear pathways for its experimental assess-
ment. However, a quantitative comparison with real strong-interaction dynamics remains
premature. Establishing such correspondence will require:
• rigorous derivation of vortex-core structure,
• numerical modeling of many-vortex systems,
• quantitative parameter fitting,
• and, most importantly, the design and realization of the proposed experimental
tests.
These tasks form the foundation of our ongoing and future research.
